\title{Small-Data Surrogate Shock-Aware Designed (S3) models: Gradient-Free Residual Adaptation with Adaptive Conformal Uncertainty for Engineering Time Series}
\author{A.patino}

\begin{abstract}
Forecasting short engineering time series is difficult because limited
observations, non-stationary dynamics, and calibration drift make
high-capacity local models unstable and can also reduce the reliability
of zero-shot priors. This study introduces Small-Data Surrogate
shock-aware design (S3), a gradient-free residual-adaptation framework
comprising two complementary architectures. S3-Forecaster combines a
frozen causal prior with a fixed Echo State Network, a Bayesian Ridge
residual readout, and a volatility-sensitive out-of-bag gate.
S3-FastSketch replaces the recurrent reservoir with deterministic causal
multiscale features, a closed-form Ridge readout, and bounded calibration
shrinkage. Both variants are coupled with Adaptive Conformal Inference
for sequential prediction intervals. The methods are evaluated under a
strict chronological train--calibration--test protocol on an anonymized
industrial construction-material demand series and four public monthly
benchmarks, with all histories containing fewer than 200 observations.
Against statistical, kernel, deep-learning, lightweight, spline-based,
and foundation-model baselines, the results show dataset- and
objective-dependent advantages rather than universal dominance.
S3-Forecaster achieves the lowest root mean squared error and highest
coefficient of determination on the industrial application, whereas
S3-FastSketch attains the lowest proportional error. On the M4 benchmark,
S3-FastSketch is the strongest point forecaster, while S3-Forecaster
yields the best adaptive conformal interval score. Ablation results show
that residual adaptation is beneficial only when prior errors contain
stable predictable structure, and that gating mainly acts as protective
shrinkage against over-correction. The proposed family therefore provides
a computationally efficient adaptation layer for short, non-stationary
engineering series without end-to-end neural retraining.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Time-series forecasting is a control-relevant inference layer in complex engineering systems because its outputs are consumed directly by production planning, automated inventory allocation, and logistics optimization modules rather than being used only as descriptive summaries \cite{park2024forchestra,li2022intermittent}. The same dependence appears in smart-grid operation and predictive maintenance, where forecast errors propagate into dispatch imbalance, downtime, reliability losses, and suboptimal asset scheduling \cite{kaur2020smartgrid,zhu2019pdm}. In modern industrial pipelines, sensor streams, SCADA records, enterprise transactions, and maintenance logs are fused into rolling forecasting services whose predictions feed downstream optimization and control loops across multiple horizons \cite{lim2021survey}. The immediate collision with reality is that many asset-level or SKU-level engineering series are not data-rich: new installations, catalog churn, sparse failures, intermittent demand, and local operating reconfigurations often leave only short and irregular histories from which the forecaster must generalize \cite{rozanec2021lumpy}.

Formally, let $\{y_t\}_{t=1}^{N}$ denote a univariate engineering series and let $h \ge 1$ be the forecast horizon. In the small-data regime considered here, $N<200$, and the effective number of supervised lag--target tuples after windowing with lag length $w$ is only
\[
M = N-w-h+1,
\]
which is frequently too small to identify a high-capacity predictor $f_\theta$ without severe estimation variance \cite{gao2023sedan,lim2021survey}. Representative state-of-the-art families range from recurrent LSTMs and efficient attention models such as Informer to patch-based Transformers such as PatchTST \cite{hochreiter1997lstm,zhou2021informer,nie2023patchtst}. At the foundation-model end, Chronos and TimesFM replace local estimation by massive global pretraining and zero-shot transfer across heterogeneous collections of time series \cite{ansari2024chronos,das2024timesfm}. However, when one isolated industrial series is used as a stand-alone training task, end-to-end estimation becomes statistically fragile because the parameter-to-sample ratio is unfavorable, regularization becomes underspecified, and local validation itself is noisy under such short histories \cite{gao2023sedan}. Benchmark evidence has consequently shown that architectural sophistication is not a sufficient condition for better forecasting, since simple linear baselines can outperform many Transformer forecasters, while non-stationary settings have been reported to induce severe overfitting in attention-based designs \cite{zeng2023dlinear,shen2023gbt}. For globally pretrained models, the prior is powerful only when source--target similarity is adequate; otherwise the imported weights can miss the microscopic local dependencies of a single engineering series and induce bias, uncertainty distortion, or outright negative transfer rather than adaptation \cite{ehrig2024similarity}.

A second bottleneck is the coexistence of non-stationary dynamics and structural shocks, which is more faithfully represented by
\[
y_t = m_t + a_t + \sigma_t \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{D}_t(0,1),
\]
where the latent level $m_t$, local amplitude $a_t$, conditional scale $\sigma_t$, and even the innovation law $\mathcal{D}_t$ may jump at unobserved break times because of maintenance interventions, control-policy modifications, promotions, supply disruptions, or exogenous demand shocks \cite{hamilton1990regime,hamilton1994arch}. Traditional remedies address this instability with smoothly evolving latent states or recursively discounted parameters, including time-varying parameter regressions, parsimonious TVP-VAR constructions, and related dynamic Bayesian formulations that are effective under gradual drift but structurally misaligned with abrupt nonlinear transitions \cite{callot2014tvpvar,fischer2021tvpvar}. A complementary line relies on dynamic model averaging, regime-switching filters, or Bayesian online changepoint detection to detect breaks and then reset or reweight predictors once sufficient post-shock evidence has accumulated \cite{onorante2014dma,adams2007bocpd}. Yet these mechanisms are evidentiary rather than instantaneous: if a break occurs at time $\tau$ and a width-$w$ adaptive estimator has seen only $k$ post-shock observations, then a stale mass $1-k/w$ of the estimator still comes from the obsolete regime, so the fitted parameters remain convex mixtures of incompatible dynamics and the forecast incurs a systematic geometric lag \cite{lu2020drift,tosi2023optwin}. Recent online-forecasting studies now identify precisely this adaptation lag as a central failure mode, since rolling updates and online ensembles can exhibit continuous performance regression whenever concept drift accumulates faster than the model can safely relearn \cite{zhang2023onenet,zhang2024d3a}. The difficulty is sharper in forecasting than in ordinary online regression because labels arrive only after the horizon closes, so the update rule may adapt to stale concepts rather than to the active operating regime, a temporal mismatch that has motivated proactive drift-compensation mechanisms \cite{zhao2024proceed}. In narrow engineering decision windows, this delay produces transient mean-squared-error amplification exactly where forecast instability is hardest to diagnose and most operationally expensive \cite{casini2018forecast,wendelberger2021bocpd}.

A third bottleneck is uncertainty quantification. In automated industrial decision-making, a point forecast $\hat{y}_{t+h \mid t}$ is rarely sufficient unless it is accompanied by a predictive interval or set
\[
C_{t,h}^{1-\alpha} = \left[\hat{y}_{t+h \mid t} - q_t^\alpha,\; \hat{y}_{t+h \mid t} + q_t^\alpha\right],
\]
whose coverage is reliable under real deployment shift rather than only under retrospective fitting \cite{angelopoulos2023conformal}. Classical parametric UQ remains useful but brittle in this operating regime: standard Gaussian-process regression assumes homoscedastic Gaussian residuals unless explicitly extended, while baseline GARCH-style volatility recursions impose a low-dimensional parametric evolution on conditional variance and do not by themselves resolve skewness, heavy tails, or break-driven distributional change \cite{wang2012gp,bollerslev1986garch}. Indeed, dedicated GP extensions are required for heteroscedastic non-stationarity, and empirical studies show that skewness and heavy tails matter materially for predictive performance in stressed regimes relative to Gaussian structural shocks \cite{tolvanen2014gp,karlsson2021skewvar}. Conformal prediction is attractive because it replaces a full parametric residual law by calibration through nonconformity scores and thereby targets distribution-free predictive validity under minimal assumptions \cite{shafer2008tutorial}. However, the basic conformal premise of exchangeability is systematically violated in sequential engineering data with deep temporal dependence and evolving regimes, so vanilla conformal calibration cannot be transferred unchanged to this setting \cite{zaffran2022adaptive,xu2023spci}. Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI) addresses this exchangeability violation by recasting miscoverage control as an online learning problem in which the calibration level is updated sequentially from recent forecast errors rather than fixed once and for all \cite{gibbs2021aci,gibbs2024online}. In practical deployment, such sequential calibration must remain strictly chronological; otherwise future residuals leak into the calibration buffer, creating information leakage and look-ahead bias in both interval assessment and model selection \cite{cerqueira2020evaluation,burkner2020lfo}.

Motivated by these three constraints, we introduce a unified forecasting family consisting of \emph{S3-Forecaster} and \emph{S3-FastSketch}; throughout this article, \emph{S3 denotes Small-Data Surrogate shock-aware design}. The design is intentionally asymmetric and can be written as
\[
\hat{y}^{\mathrm{S3}}_{t \mid t-1}
=
\hat{y}^{\mathrm{base}}_{t \mid t-1}
+
g_{t-1}\,\hat{\Delta}_{t \mid t-1},
\qquad 0 \le g_{t-1} \le 1,
\]
where a causal base predictor is frozen, $\hat{\Delta}_{t \mid t-1}$ is a gradient-free residual surrogate, and $g_{t-1}$ is a shock-aware contraction factor. First, this directly attacks the small-data bottleneck because S3-Forecaster computes the residual correction from a fixed-reservoir Echo State Network whose fading-memory dynamics are regulated by spectral radius constraints, while leaving only the readout trainable \cite{jaeger2004science,armenio2019esn}. S3-FastSketch follows the same low-variance philosophy without recurrence, replacing the reservoir by deterministic multiscale causal features and a convex linear decoder, a design aligned with the growing evidence that carefully constrained linear or decomposition-aware correctors can remain highly competitive when local data are scarce \cite{zeng2023dlinear,gao2023sedan}. In both variants, the trainable stage is reduced to a convex readout, so the corrective mechanism inherits convex optimization stability rather than the nonconvex fragility of backpropagation through a deep sequence model \cite{lukosevicius2009reservoir}. Second, both variants are made shock-aware through out-of-bag calibration gates or bounded shrinkage factors that suppress residual over-correction immediately after abrupt transitions, thereby targeting adaptation lag without repeatedly refitting a large network after each shift \cite{zhang2024d3a,zhao2024proceed}. Third, uncertainty is handled by a sequential ACI layer that updates interval width from recent nonconformity scores and seeks coverage robustness under drift without assuming Gaussianity, homoscedasticity, or exchangeability \cite{gibbs2021aci,xu2023spci}. By separating training, calibration, and deployment chronologically, the proposed S3 family is constructed to minimize information leakage, eliminate look-ahead bias, and reduce negative transfer from obsolete pre-shock segments while preserving an operationally light forecasting workflow \cite{cerqueira2020evaluation,burkner2020lfo}.


\subsection{Key Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item We formulate small-data engineering forecasting as a \emph{surrogate residual adaptation} problem and propose a gradient-free correction architecture that freezes the baseline forecaster and learns only a low-capacity residual readout, thereby prioritizing variance control and convex optimization stability over end-to-end parameter proliferation \cite{zeng2023dlinear,gao2023sedan}.
    \item We introduce two complementary model realizations---\emph{S3-Forecaster}, based on fixed-reservoir Echo State dynamics under spectral radius constraints, and \emph{S3-FastSketch}, based on deterministic causal multiscale sketches---to separate temporal representation from lightweight residual decoding in genuinely short-series regimes \cite{jaeger2004science,armenio2019esn,maass2002reservoir}.
    \item We develop a shock-aware residual control mechanism based on out-of-bag gating and bounded shrinkage, motivated by the documented adaptation lag of classical online updaters under concept drift and by the need to suppress variance amplification when apparent residual structure is not stable enough to be learnable \cite{zhang2023onenet,zhang2024d3a}.
    \item We integrate a sequential Adaptive Conformal Inference layer for uncertainty quantification, so that prediction intervals are calibrated by recent nonconformity scores and retain distribution-free predictive validity despite exchangeability violation in dependent engineering sequences \cite{gibbs2021aci,xu2023spci,angelopoulos2023conformal}.
    \item We adopt a strict chronological train--calibration--test protocol designed to preserve temporal order and avoid information leakage and look-ahead bias, aligning evaluation with the actual decision task faced in forecast deployment rather than with i.i.d.\ cross-validation surrogates \cite{cerqueira2020evaluation,burkner2020lfo}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{subsec:problem_formulation}

Let
\begin{equation}
    \bm{y}_{1:N}
    =
    \left(y_1,\ldots,y_N\right)^\top
    \in \R^N
\end{equation}
denote a univariate engineering time series with monthly sampling frequency and
limited historical availability, $N<200$. The forecasting problem is defined
with respect to the natural filtration
\begin{equation}
    \Fcal_t
    =
    \sigma(y_1,\ldots,y_t),
\end{equation}
which represents all target information observed up to time $t$. A valid
one-step-ahead predictor must satisfy
\begin{equation}
    \yhat_{t|t-1}
    \in
    \Fcal_{t-1},
\end{equation}
such that $y_t$ cannot influence its own forecast.
The proposed family follows a residual-adaptation decomposition:
\begin{equation}
    \yhat_{t|t-1}
    =
    \ybase_{t|t-1}
    +
    \widehat{\Delta}_{t|t-1},
    \label{eq:general_decomposition}
\end{equation}
where $\ybase_{t|t-1}$ is generated by a frozen causal prior and
$\widehat{\Delta}_{t|t-1}$ is a data-efficient residual correction. The causal
base residual is
\begin{equation}
    e_t
    =
    y_t-\ybase_{t|t-1}.
    \label{eq:base_residual}
\end{equation}

The proposed models differ only in the representation used to estimate the
predictable component of $e_t$:
\begin{align}
    \text{S3-Forecaster:}
    \quad&
    e_{1:t-1}
    \longmapsto
    \bm{h}_{t-1}
    \longmapsto
    \rhat_{t|t-1},
    \\
    \text{S3-FastSketch:}
    \quad&
    e_{1:t-1}
    \longmapsto
    \bm{\phi}_{t-1}
    \longmapsto
    \rhat_{t|t-1}.
\end{align}

Neither architecture backpropagates through the causal prior or through its
nonlinear feature extractor. The learned component is restricted to a
regularized linear readout and a low-dimensional calibration mechanism.

\subsection{Frozen Causal Forecast Prior}
\label{subsec:causal_prior}

Let
\begin{equation}
    f_{\mathrm{base}}:
    \bigcup_{t\geq1}\R^t
    \longrightarrow
    \R
\end{equation}
denote a statistical, foundation-model, or heuristic forecasting operator whose
parameters are frozen during residual adaptation. Its strictly causal output is
\begin{equation}
    \ybase_{t|t-1}
    =
    f_{\mathrm{base}}
    \left(
    y_1,\ldots,y_{t-1};
    \bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{base}}
    \right).
    \label{eq:causal_base}
\end{equation}

The generic formulation permits the use of rolling estimators, ETS, Prophet,
ARIMA, or a zero-shot time-series foundation model. The residual adapter does
not alter $\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{base}}$. Consequently, adaptation cost is
independent of the number of parameters in the frozen prior.
For the lightweight default prior, a causal rolling level estimate is defined
as
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\mu}_{t-1}^{(w)}
    =
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{W}_{t-1}|}
    \sum_{j\in\mathcal{W}_{t-1}}y_j,
    \qquad
    \mathcal{W}_{t-1}
    =
    \left\{
    \max(1,t-w),\ldots,t-1
    \right\}.
\end{equation}

When sufficient monthly history is available, a causal seasonal correction may
be added:
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{s}_{m(t)}
    =
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{m(t)}|}
    \sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{m(t)}}
    \left(y_j-\widehat{\mu}_{j-1}^{(w)}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{I}_{m(t)}$ contains only pre-$t$ observations belonging to the
same calendar month as $t$. The resulting prior is
\begin{equation}
    \ybase_{t|t-1}
    =
    \widehat{\mu}_{t-1}^{(w)}
    +
    \widehat{s}_{m(t)}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{S3-Forecaster: Fixed Reservoir Residual Adaptation}
\label{subsec:s3_forecaster}

\subsubsection{Residual Normalization}

The residual stream is standardized using statistics estimated exclusively
from the training block:
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{e}_t
    =
    \frac{e_t-\widehat{m}_{e}}
    {\widehat{q}_{0.75}(e)-\widehat{q}_{0.25}(e)+\eps_s},
    \qquad
    t\in\Dtrain,
    \label{eq:residual_scaling}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat{m}_{e}$ is the training median and $\eps_s>0$ prevents division
by zero. The same frozen transformation is used for calibration and testing.

\subsubsection{Echo State Representation}

Let $R$ denote the reservoir dimension. The recurrent state is
\begin{equation}
    \bm{h}_t
    \in
    [-1,1]^R.
\end{equation}
A leaky ESN update is defined by
\begin{align}
    \widetilde{\bm{h}}_t
    &=
    \tanh
    \left(
        \bm{W}_{\mathrm{in}}
        \begin{bmatrix}
            1\\
            \widetilde{e}_t
        \end{bmatrix}
        +
        \bm{W}_{\mathrm{res}}\bm{h}_{t-1}
    \right),
    \label{eq:esn_candidate}
    \\
    \bm{h}_t
    &=
    (1-\lambda_{\mathrm{leak}})\bm{h}_{t-1}
    +
    \lambda_{\mathrm{leak}}\widetilde{\bm{h}}_t,
    \qquad
    \lambda_{\mathrm{leak}}\in(0,1].
    \label{eq:esn_state}
\end{align}

The fixed matrices satisfy
\begin{align}
    \bm{W}_{\mathrm{in}}
    &\in
    \R^{R\times2},
    \\
    \bm{W}_{\mathrm{res}}
    &\in
    \R^{R\times R}.
\end{align}
They are sampled once from seed-controlled random distributions and are not
updated during training.
Let $\widetilde{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{res}}$ be an initially sampled recurrent
matrix. Spectral normalization is performed as
\begin{equation}
    \bm{W}_{\mathrm{res}}
    =
    \rho_\star
    \frac{
        \widetilde{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{res}}
    }{
        \rho\!\left(
        \widetilde{\bm{W}}_{\mathrm{res}}
        \right)+\eps_\rho
    },
    \qquad
    0<\rho_\star<1,
    \label{eq:spectral_normalization}
\end{equation}
where $\rho(\cdot)$ denotes spectral radius.
The stronger contraction condition
\begin{equation}
    \left\|
    \bm{W}_{\mathrm{res}}
    \right\|_2
    <1
\end{equation}
is sufficient for asymptotic forgetting of initial states under a
$1$-Lipschitz activation. In practice,
$\rho(\bm{W}_{\mathrm{res}})<1$ is used as the standard reservoir-design
heuristic. Its purpose is to prevent uncontrolled recurrent amplification while
retaining a fading memory of the residual history.
To supplement the nonlinear reservoir with explicit short-term information, an
autoregressive residual vector is constructed:
\begin{equation}
    \bm{\ell}_t^{(p)}
    =
    \left[
    e_t,\,
    e_{t-1},\,
    \ldots,\,
    e_{t-p+1}
    \right]^\top
    \in\R^p.
\end{equation}

The complete S3 representation is
\begin{equation}
    \bm{z}_t
    =
    \left[
    1,\,
    \bm{h}_t^\top,\,
    \left(\bm{\ell}_t^{(p)}\right)^\top
    \right]^\top
    \in
    \R^{P},
    \qquad
    P=1+R+p.
    \label{eq:s3_representation}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Bayesian Ridge Residual Readout}

For one-step-ahead forecasting, the readout assumes
\begin{equation}
    e_{t+1}
    =
    \bm{w}^\top\bm{z}_t
    +
    \xi_{t+1},
    \qquad
    \xi_{t+1}
    \sim
    \mathcal{N}(0,\beta^{-1}).
    \label{eq:bayesian_likelihood}
\end{equation}

A zero-mean isotropic Gaussian prior is imposed:
\begin{equation}
    p(\bm{w}\mid\alpha)
    =
    \mathcal{N}
    \left(
    \bm{0},
    \alpha^{-1}\bm{I}_{P}
    \right).
    \label{eq:ridge_prior}
\end{equation}

Let
\begin{align}
    \bm{Z}
    &=
    \left[
    \bm{z}_{t_1},\,
    \ldots,\,
    \bm{z}_{t_n}
    \right]^\top
    \in
    \R^{n\times P},
    \\
    \bm{r}
    &=
    \left[
    e_{t_1+1},\,
    \ldots,\,
    e_{t_n+1}
    \right]^\top
    \in
    \R^n.
\end{align}

The negative log-posterior, excluding constants independent of $\bm{w}$, is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{J}(\bm{w})
    =
    \frac{\beta}{2}
    \left\|
    \bm{r}-\bm{Z}\bm{w}
    \right\|_2^2
    +
    \frac{\alpha}{2}
    \left\|
    \bm{w}
    \right\|_2^2.
    \label{eq:ridge_objective}
\end{equation}

The Hessian is
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_{\bm{w}}^2
    \mathcal{J}(\bm{w})
    =
    \beta\bm{Z}^\top\bm{Z}
    +
    \alpha\bm{I}_{P}
    \succ0
\end{equation}
for $\alpha>0$. Therefore, the objective is strictly convex and has a unique
global minimizer.
Setting the gradient to zero gives
\begin{align}
    \nabla_{\bm{w}}\mathcal{J}(\bm{w})
    &=
    -\beta\bm{Z}^\top
    \left(
    \bm{r}-\bm{Z}\bm{w}
    \right)
    +
    \alpha\bm{w}
    =
    \bm{0},
    \\
    \left(
    \beta\bm{Z}^\top\bm{Z}
    +
    \alpha\bm{I}_{P}
    \right)\bm{w}
    &=
    \beta\bm{Z}^\top\bm{r}.
\end{align}

Hence,
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\bm{w}}_{\mathrm{MAP}}
    =
    \left(
    \beta\bm{Z}^\top\bm{Z}
    +
    \alpha\bm{I}_{P}
    \right)^{-1}
    \beta\bm{Z}^\top\bm{r}.
    \label{eq:ridge_closed_form}
\end{equation}

Equivalently, defining $\lambda_R=\alpha/\beta$,
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\bm{w}}_{\mathrm{MAP}}
    =
    \left(
    \bm{Z}^\top\bm{Z}
    +
    \lambda_R\bm{I}_{P}
    \right)^{-1}
    \bm{Z}^\top\bm{r}.
\end{equation}

The complete posterior is
\begin{align}
    p(\bm{w}\mid\bm{r},\bm{Z},\alpha,\beta)
    &=
    \mathcal{N}
    \left(
    \bm{\mu}_w,
    \bm{\Sigma}_w
    \right),
    \\
    \bm{\Sigma}_w
    &=
    \left(
    \alpha\bm{I}_{P}
    +
    \beta\bm{Z}^\top\bm{Z}
    \right)^{-1},
    \\
    \bm{\mu}_w
    &=
    \beta
    \bm{\Sigma}_w
    \bm{Z}^\top\bm{r}.
\end{align}

The one-step residual correction is
\begin{equation}
    \rhat_{t+1|t}
    =
    \bm{\mu}_w^\top\bm{z}_t.
    \label{eq:s3_raw_residual}
\end{equation}

The Bayesian derivation provides a probabilistic interpretation of the
regularized readout. When fixed precision parameters are used, its posterior
mode is numerically equivalent to conventional Ridge regression. Lasso and
Elastic Net may be considered as convex sensitivity variants, but they must not
be described as Bayesian Ridge unless the corresponding prior interpretation is
explicitly introduced.

\subsubsection{Volatility-Sensitive OOB Gate}

The residual correction is not assumed to be uniformly reliable. A causal
volatility process is therefore computed from the prior residuals. Let
\begin{align}
    \mu_t^{(e)}
    &=
    (1-\lambda_v)\mu_{t-1}^{(e)}
    +
    \lambda_v e_t,
    \\
    q_t^{(e)}
    &=
    (1-\lambda_v)q_{t-1}^{(e)}
    +
    \lambda_v
    \left(
    e_t-\mu_{t-1}^{(e)}
    \right)^2,
    \\
    v_t
    &=
    \sqrt{q_t^{(e)}+\eps_v}.
    \label{eq:causal_volatility}
\end{align}

The volatility is robustly standardized using training-only statistics:
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{v}_t
    =
    \frac{
        v_t-\operatorname{median}_{j\in\Dtrain}(v_j)
    }{
        \operatorname{IQR}_{j\in\Dtrain}(v_j)/1.349+\eps_v
    }.
    \label{eq:volatility_standardization}
\end{equation}

The gate is
\begin{equation}
    g_t(a,b)
    =
    \sigma
    \left(
    a(\widetilde{v}_t-b)
    \right)
    =
    \frac{
        1
    }{
        1+\exp[-a(\widetilde{v}_t-b)]
    },
    \qquad
    g_t\in(0,1).
    \label{eq:s3_gate}
\end{equation}

The ESN and its readout are fitted on $\Dtrain$. Keeping these components fixed,
the two gate parameters are estimated exclusively on the chronological
calibration block:
\begin{equation}
    (\widehat{a},\widehat{b})
    =
    \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{
        a\in[a_{\min},a_{\max}],
        \,
        b\in[b_{\min},b_{\max}]
    }
    \frac{1}{|\Dcal|}
    \sum_{t\in\Dcal}
    \left|
        e_t
        -
        g_{t-1}(a,b)
        \rhat_{t|t-1}
    \right|.
    \label{eq:oob_gate_optimization}
\end{equation}

This is an out-of-bag optimization with respect to the readout because no
calibration observation is used to estimate $\bm{w}$. The gate is a
two-parameter calibration mechanism rather than a second high-capacity
predictor.
The final S3 point forecast is
\begin{equation}
    \yhat^{\mathrm{S3}}_{t|t-1}
    =
    \ybase_{t|t-1}
    +
    g_{t-1}(\widehat{a},\widehat{b})
    \rhat_{t|t-1}.
    \label{eq:s3_final_forecast}
\end{equation}

The term \emph{gradient-free} refers to the absence of end-to-end
backpropagation through the causal prior and reservoir. The bounded
low-dimensional calibration of $(a,b)$ is not conflated with neural
gradient-based fine-tuning.

\subsection{S3-FastSketch: Lightweight Multiscale Residual Adaptation}
\label{subsec:fastsketch}

\subsubsection{Causal Multiscale Feature Map}

S3-FastSketch replaces the recurrent reservoir by a deterministic causal map
\begin{equation}
    \Phi:
    e_{1:t}
    \longmapsto
    \bm{\phi}_t
    \in\R^{D_\phi}.
\end{equation}

The representation is
\begin{equation}
    \bm{\phi}_t
    =
    \left[
    \bm{\ell}_t^\top,\,
    \bm{a}_t^\top,\,
    \bm{v}_t^\top,\,
    \bm{d}_t^\top,\,
    \bm{c}_t^\top,\,
    \bm{s}_t^\top
    \right]^\top.
    \label{eq:fast_feature_map}
\end{equation}

Let $\mathcal{Q}=\{q_1,\ldots,q_Q\}$ be a set of causal smoothing spans and
$\mathcal{K}=\{k_1,\ldots,k_K\}$ a set of local filter scales.

\paragraph{Residual lag block.}

For $p$ autoregressive lags,
\begin{equation}
    \bm{\ell}_t
    =
    \left[
    e_{t-1},
    e_{t-2},
    \ldots,
    e_{t-p}
    \right]^\top
    \in\R^p.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Exponentially weighted residual block.}

For each $q\in\mathcal{Q}$,
\begin{align}
    a_t^{(q)}
    &=
    (1-\eta_q)a_{t-1}^{(q)}
    +
    \eta_qe_t,
    \\
    \eta_q
    &=
    \frac{2}{q+1}.
\end{align}
The block is
\begin{equation}
    \bm{a}_t
    =
    \left[
    a_t^{(q_1)},\ldots,a_t^{(q_Q)}
    \right]^\top.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Residual-magnitude block.}

The multiscale magnitude summaries are
\begin{equation}
    v_t^{(q)}
    =
    (1-\eta_q)v_{t-1}^{(q)}
    +
    \eta_q|e_t|,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \bm{v}_t
    =
    \left[
    v_t^{(q_1)},\ldots,v_t^{(q_Q)}
    \right]^\top.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Local-difference block.}

For $q\in\mathcal{Q}$,
\begin{equation}
    d_t^{(q)}
    =
    \begin{cases}
        e_t-e_{t-q}, & t>q,\\
        0, & t\leq q.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Thus,
\begin{equation}
    \bm{d}_t
    =
    \left[
    d_t^{(q_1)},\ldots,d_t^{(q_Q)}
    \right]^\top.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Fixed causal convolutional block.}

For each $k\in\mathcal{K}$, define the causal window
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{W}_{t,k}
    =
    \left\{
    \max(1,t-k+1),\ldots,t
    \right\}.
\end{equation}

Its local mean is
\begin{equation}
    c_{t,k}^{(\mathrm{mean})}
    =
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{W}_{t,k}|}
    \sum_{j\in\mathcal{W}_{t,k}}e_j.
\end{equation}

Its endpoint slope is
\begin{equation}
    c_{t,k}^{(\mathrm{slope})}
    =
    e_t-e_{\max(1,t-k+1)}.
\end{equation}

Let $\mathcal{W}_{t,k}^{L}$ and $\mathcal{W}_{t,k}^{R}$ denote the first and
second halves of $\mathcal{W}_{t,k}$. The local contrast is
\begin{equation}
    c_{t,k}^{(\mathrm{contrast})}
    =
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{W}_{t,k}^{R}|}
    \sum_{j\in\mathcal{W}_{t,k}^{R}}e_j
    -
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{W}_{t,k}^{L}|}
    \sum_{j\in\mathcal{W}_{t,k}^{L}}e_j.
\end{equation}

The local absolute energy is
\begin{equation}
    c_{t,k}^{(\mathrm{energy})}
    =
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{W}_{t,k}|}
    \sum_{j\in\mathcal{W}_{t,k}}|e_j|.
\end{equation}

The convolutional block is the concatenation
\begin{equation}
    \bm{c}_t
    =
    \operatorname{concat}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
    \left[
    c_{t,k}^{(\mathrm{mean})},
    c_{t,k}^{(\mathrm{slope})},
    c_{t,k}^{(\mathrm{contrast})},
    c_{t,k}^{(\mathrm{energy})}
    \right].
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Seasonal and calendar block.}

For monthly seasonality $m=12$,
\begin{equation}
    \bm{s}_t
    =
    \left[
    e_{t-m},\,
    e_t-e_{t-m},\,
    \sin\left(\frac{2\pi t}{m}\right),\,
    \cos\left(\frac{2\pi t}{m}\right)
    \right]^\top,
    \label{eq:seasonal_sketch}
\end{equation}
where unavailable seasonal lags are initialized to zero.
Each feature dimension is standardized using training medians and interquartile
ranges:
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{\phi}_{t,j}
    =
    \frac{
        \phi_{t,j}
        -
        \operatorname{median}_{u\in\Dtrain}(\phi_{u,j})
    }{
        \operatorname{IQR}_{u\in\Dtrain}(\phi_{u,j})/1.349+\eps_\phi
    }.
    \label{eq:fast_robust_scaling}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Regularized Residual Readout}

For a forecast horizon $H$, define
\begin{equation}
    \bm{r}_{t+1:t+H}
    =
    \left[
    e_{t+1},\ldots,e_{t+H}
    \right]^\top
    \in\R^H.
\end{equation}

The multivariate residual readout is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\bm{r}}_{t+1:t+H|t}
    =
    \bm{W}^{\top}
    \widetilde{\bm{\phi}}_t,
    \qquad
    \bm{W}\in\R^{D_\phi\times H}.
\end{equation}

Let
\begin{align}
    \bm{\Phi}
    &\in
    \R^{n_{\mathrm{tr}}\times D_\phi},
    \\
    \bm{R}
    &\in
    \R^{n_{\mathrm{tr}}\times H}
\end{align}
contain the training sketches and future residual targets. The readout solves
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\bm{W}}
    =
    \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\bm{W}}
    \left\|
    \bm{R}-\bm{\Phi}\bm{W}
    \right\|_F^2
    +
    \lambda_F
    \left\|
    \bm{W}
    \right\|_F^2.
    \label{eq:fast_ridge_objective}
\end{equation}

Its closed-form solution is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\bm{W}}
    =
    \left(
    \bm{\Phi}^\top\bm{\Phi}
    +
    \lambda_F\bm{I}_{D_\phi}
    \right)^{-1}
    \bm{\Phi}^\top\bm{R}.
    \label{eq:fast_ridge_solution}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Projected Calibration Shrinkage}

The raw residual forecast may be unstable when the calibration block is small.
For each horizon $h$, a single bounded coefficient is therefore estimated from
calibration pairs
\begin{equation}
    \left\{
    \left(
    r_{i,h},
    \widehat{r}_{i,h}
    \right)
    \right\}_{i\in\Dcal}.
\end{equation}

The calibration objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_h(\gamma)
    =
    \sum_{i\in\Dcal}
    \left(
    r_{i,h}
    -
    \gamma\widehat{r}_{i,h}
    \right)^2.
    \label{eq:gamma_loss}
\end{equation}

Differentiating gives
\begin{align}
    \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_h}{\partial\gamma}
    &=
    -2
    \sum_{i\in\Dcal}
    \widehat{r}_{i,h}
    \left(
    r_{i,h}
    -
    \gamma\widehat{r}_{i,h}
    \right),
    \\
    0
    &=
    \sum_{i\in\Dcal}
    \widehat{r}_{i,h}r_{i,h}
    -
    \gamma
    \sum_{i\in\Dcal}
    \widehat{r}_{i,h}^2.
\end{align}

The unconstrained solution is
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{\gamma}_h
    =
    \frac{
        \displaystyle
        \sum_{i\in\Dcal}
        \widehat{r}_{i,h}r_{i,h}
    }{
        \displaystyle
        \sum_{i\in\Dcal}
        \widehat{r}_{i,h}^2+\eps_\gamma
    }.
    \label{eq:fast_gamma_raw}
\end{equation}

The final coefficient is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\gamma}_h
    =
    \Pi_{[0,\gamma_{\max}]}
    \left(
    \widetilde{\gamma}_h
    \right),
    \label{eq:fast_gamma_projected}
\end{equation}
where the Euclidean projection is
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_{[0,\gamma_{\max}]}(x)
    =
    \min
    \left\{
    \gamma_{\max},
    \max\{0,x\}
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:projection_operator}
\end{equation}

The lower boundary suppresses a residual correction whose calibration
association is non-positive, whereas the upper boundary prevents excessive
amplification. Only one scalar is estimated per horizon, thereby limiting
calibration variance without requiring nonlinear gate optimization.
The FastSketch forecast is
\begin{equation}
    \yhat^{\mathrm{FS}}_{t+h|t}
    =
    \ybase_{t+h|t}
    +
    \widehat{\gamma}_h
    \widehat{r}_{t+h|t},
    \qquad
    h=1,\ldots,H.
    \label{eq:fast_final_forecast}
\end{equation}

The reported experiments use $H=1$. The multi-horizon notation is retained to
show that the architecture extends directly by assigning an independent
readout column and shrinkage coefficient to each horizon.

\subsection{Adaptive Conformal Uncertainty Estimation}
\label{subsec:aci}

The same uncertainty-estimation layer is applied after either S3-Forecaster or
S3-FastSketch. Let $\yhat_{t|t-1}$ denote the final point forecast. The
nonconformity score is
\begin{equation}
    s_t
    =
    \left|
    y_t-\yhat_{t|t-1}
    \right|.
    \label{eq:nonconformity}
\end{equation}

For $H>1$, a simultaneous score can be defined as
\begin{equation}
    s_t^{(H)}
    =
    \max_{h=1,\ldots,H}
    \left|
    y_{t+h}-\yhat_{t+h|t}
    \right|.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Dynamic Score Buffer}

Let $W$ denote the maximum calibration-buffer length. Immediately before
forecasting $y_t$, the score buffer is
\begin{equation}
    \Scal_t
    =
    \left\{
    s_j:
    \max(t-W,t_{\mathrm{cal},1})
    \leq j\leq t-1
    \right\},
    \label{eq:aci_buffer}
\end{equation}
where $t_{\mathrm{cal},1}$ is the first calibration index. Thus,
\begin{equation}
    |\Scal_t|
    \leq W.
\end{equation}

Given an effective miscoverage level $\delta_t$, define
\begin{equation}
    k_t
    =
    \min
    \left\{
    |\Scal_t|,
    \left\lceil
    (|\Scal_t|+1)(1-\delta_t)
    \right\rceil
    \right\}.
\end{equation}

The finite-buffer empirical quantile is
\begin{equation}
    q_t(\delta_t)
    =
    s_{(k_t)}(\Scal_t),
    \label{eq:aci_quantile}
\end{equation}
where $s_{(k_t)}(\Scal_t)$ is the $k_t$-th order statistic.
The adaptive conformal interval is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{C}_t
    =
    \left[
    \yhat_{t|t-1}-q_t(\delta_t),
    \;
    \yhat_{t|t-1}+q_t(\delta_t)
    \right].
    \label{eq:aci_interval}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Sequential Miscoverage Update}

After observing $y_t$, define
\begin{equation}
    M_t
    =
    \Ind
    \left\{
    y_t\notin\widehat{C}_t
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:miss_indicator}
\end{equation}

Let $\delta^\star$ be the target miscoverage, with
$\delta^\star=0.10$ for nominal $90\%$ coverage. The effective miscoverage is
updated as
\begin{equation}
    \delta_{t+1}
    =
    \Pi_{[\delta_{\min},\delta_{\max}]}
    \left[
        \delta_t
        +
        \eta_{\mathrm{ACI}}
        \left(
        \delta^\star-M_t
        \right)
    \right],
    \label{eq:aci_update}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_{[\delta_{\min},\delta_{\max}]}(x)
    =
    \min
    \left\{
    \delta_{\max},
    \max\{\delta_{\min},x\}
    \right\}.
\end{equation}

When a miss occurs, $M_t=1$, the update decreases $\delta_{t+1}$ and therefore
raises the next empirical quantile, producing a wider interval. When coverage
is repeatedly achieved, $\delta_t$ can increase gradually, reducing excessive
width. Finally,
\begin{equation}
    \Scal_{t+1}
    =
    \operatorname{Tail}_{W}
    \left(
    \Scal_t\cup\{s_t\}
    \right).
    \label{eq:aci_buffer_update}
\end{equation}

This construction does not assume a Gaussian residual distribution. Its
distribution-free interpretation concerns the absence of a parametric error
law under conformal validity conditions. Under serial dependence and temporal
drift, ACI targets adaptive long-run miscoverage control; it does not imply
exact conditional coverage for every individual forecast or every short test
block.

\subsection{Model Complexity}
\label{subsec:model_complexity}

For S3-Forecaster, the number of learned readout coefficients is approximately
\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{S3}}
    =
    H(1+R+p)
    +
    2,
\end{equation}
where the final two parameters correspond to the OOB gate. The fixed matrices
$\bm{W}_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $\bm{W}_{\mathrm{res}}$ are excluded because they are
not trained.

For S3-FastSketch,
\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{FS}}
    =
    H D_\phi+H,
\end{equation}
where the final $H$ coefficients are the calibration shrinkage factors.
For a dense reservoir, state propagation requires
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{O}(NR^2)
\end{equation}
operations, which can be reduced using a sparse recurrent matrix. FastSketch
feature construction requires approximately
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{O}(ND_\phi).
\end{equation}

Both methods avoid iterative backpropagation through a nonlinear sequence
model. Their learned readouts are obtained through convex regularized
objectives, while FastSketch additionally avoids nonlinear gate calibration.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental_setup}

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{subsec:datasets}

The evaluation comprises one proprietary engineering application and four
public monthly forecasting collections. All evaluated series satisfy
$N<200$ and preserve their original chronological order.

\subsubsection{Industrial Construction-Materials Demand}

The primary engineering application is an anonymized
\emph{Industrial Construction-Materials Demand} dataset, denoted ICMD. The
source database contains $61{,}395$ transactional records, $47$ original
variables, $555$ product references, and $62$ monthly periods between January
2020 and February 2025.

The database is sparse at product level: many references are active during only
a limited subset of months. This characteristic makes global high-capacity
models difficult to estimate for individual products and motivates an
architecture with controlled trainable complexity.
The target series was constructed by aggregating positive sales value for a
selected construction-material reference:
\begin{equation}
    y_t
    =
    \sum_{j\in\mathcal{T}_t}
    \operatorname{SalesValue}_j,
    \label{eq:industrial_aggregation}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{T}_t$ is the set of valid transactions assigned to month $t$.
The evaluated contiguous series contains $48$ monthly observations from January
2020 to December 2023. The company identity and product identifier are withheld
for confidentiality, whereas the target definition, aggregation procedure,
temporal split, and model implementation remain fully specified.

\subsubsection{Public Benchmark Series}

The public evaluation includes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{M4 Monthly}: a monthly series drawn from the M4 forecasting
    competition, used as the principal reproducible methodological validation
    \citep{makridakis2020m4};

    \item \textbf{M3 Monthly}: a monthly series from the M3 forecasting
    competition \citep{makridakis2000m3};

    \item \textbf{Tourism Monthly}: a monthly tourism-demand series from the
    Tourism Forecasting Competition \citep{athanasopoulos2011tourism};

    \item \textbf{CIF 2016}: a monthly banking-domain series from the
    Computational Intelligence in Forecasting competition
    \citep{stepnicka2017cif}.
\end{itemize}

A fixed random seed and predefined eligibility rules were used for public-series
selection. Eligibility required monthly frequency, finite observations, a

\subsubsection{Preprocessing and Data Integrity}

All series were converted to a regular monthly index and sorted
chronologically. Duplicate timestamps were aggregated before splitting.
Transformations requiring estimated statistics were fitted exclusively on the
training block and then reused without re-estimation on calibration and test
observations.
Let $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{prep}}$ denote any scaling or variance-stabilizing
transformation. The causal preprocessing requirement is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{prep}}
    =
    \mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{prep}}
    \left(
    \{y_t:t\in\Dtrain\}
    \right),
\end{equation}
and not
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{prep}}
    =
    \mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{prep}}
    \left(
    \{y_t:t\in\Dtrain\cup\Dcal\cup\Dtest\}
    \right).
\end{equation}

Forecasts were inverse-transformed before computing the primary test metrics,
so all benchmark values are reported on the original application scale.

\subsection{Baseline Models}
\label{subsec:baselines}

The proposed methods are compared with ten reference models spanning five
forecasting paradigms.

\subsubsection{Statistical Models}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{AutoRegressive model (AR).}
    A finite lag model
    \begin{equation}
        y_t
        =
        c
        +
        \sum_{j=1}^{p}\varphi_jy_{t-j}
        +
        \epsilon_t
    \end{equation}
    provides a low-complexity linear benchmark.

    \item \textbf{ARIMA.}
    The model satisfies
    \begin{equation}
        \Phi(B)(1-B)^dy_t
        =
        c+\Theta(B)\epsilon_t,
    \end{equation}
    where $B$ is the lag operator and $(p,d,q)$ is selected from a bounded
    dataset-level candidate set.

    \item \textbf{ETS.}
    Exponential smoothing is evaluated using combinations of additive error,
    trend, damping, and monthly seasonal components. Seasonal terms are
    disabled when the available history is insufficient for reliable
    estimation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Classical Machine-Learning Models}

\begin{enumerate}
    \setcounter{enumi}{3}

    \item \textbf{Kernel Ridge Regression (KRR).}
    Lag windows are mapped through a kernel
    $k(\bm{x}_i,\bm{x}_j)$ and fitted using dual Ridge regularization.

    \item \textbf{Gaussian Process Regression (GP).}
    Direct lag-to-horizon mappings are estimated with RBF or Matérn covariance
    functions and an additive white-noise component.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Deep-Learning Models}

\begin{enumerate}
    \setcounter{enumi}{5}

    \item \textbf{Long Short-Term Memory network (LSTM).}
    One- or two-layer recurrent architectures are trained on chronological lag
    windows with early stopping and no shuffling.

    \item \textbf{Causal one-dimensional CNN.}
    Causal convolutional layers extract local temporal patterns, followed by a
    dense direct-forecast head.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Modern Lightweight and KAN Architectures}

\begin{enumerate}
    \setcounter{enumi}{7}

    \item \textbf{AR-KAN.}
    An autoregressive lag representation is processed by a
    Kolmogorov--Arnold Network with learnable spline-based transformations.

    \item \textbf{EasyTSF/NLinear.}
    A lightweight linear forecasting model predicts deviations from the last
    observed value:
    \begin{equation}
        \widehat{y}_{t+1}
        =
        y_t
        +
        \bm{w}^\top
        \left(
        \bm{x}_t-y_t\bm{1}
        \right).
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Time-Series Foundation Model}

\begin{enumerate}
    \setcounter{enumi}{9}

    \item \textbf{Chronos.}
    Chronos is evaluated in zero-shot mode with a frozen pretrained checkpoint
    and without gradient-based adaptation to the target series
    \citep{ansari2024chronos}.
\end{enumerate}

% windows are reported as unavailable. They are not assigned artificial worst
% scores and are excluded from within-dataset rankings.

\subsection{Offline Hyperparameter Selection and Frozen Operational Reuse}
\label{subsec:hpo}

Hyperparameter selection and operational evaluation are treated as two separate
computational phases.
For model $m$ and dataset $d$, let
\begin{equation}
    \Gcal_{m,d}
    =
    \left\{
    \bm{\theta}_{m,d}^{(1)},
    \ldots,
    \bm{\theta}_{m,d}^{(K)}
    \right\}
\end{equation}
denote a finite dataset-level micro-grid. The selected configuration is
\begin{equation}
    \bm{\theta}_{m,d}^{\star}
    =
    \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{
    \bm{\theta}\in\Gcal_{m,d}
    }
    \frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{d}^{\mathrm{dev}}|}
    \sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{d}^{\mathrm{dev}}}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}
    \left(
    m,\bm{\theta};\bm{y}^{(i)}
    \right),
    \label{eq:dataset_hpo}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{I}_{d}^{\mathrm{dev}}$ contains only development series or
pre-test development partitions from dataset $d$.
Point-forecast configurations are selected using validation
$\MAPEe$. Uncertainty parameters are selected using
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{UQ}}
    =
    \MSIS
    +
    \lambda_{\mathrm{cov}}
    \left[
    \max
    \left(
    0,
    (1-\delta^\star)-\ECP
    \right)
    \right]^2.
    \label{eq:uq_hpo_objective}
\end{equation}

After selection, $\bm{\theta}_{m,d}^{\star}$ is serialized and reused without
modification in the final benchmark, ablation, data-efficiency, and multi-prior
experiments. No test observation contributes to Eq.~\eqref{eq:dataset_hpo}.
The principal configuration dimensions are summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:hpo_dimensions}.

\subsection{Chronological Validation and Rolling Evaluation}
\label{subsec:temporal_protocol}

For a series of length $N$, define
\begin{align}
    n_{\mathrm{tr}}
    &=
    \left\lfloor0.64N\right\rfloor,
    \\
    n_{\mathrm{dev}}
    &=
    \left\lfloor0.80N\right\rfloor,
    \\
    n_{\mathrm{cal}}
    &=
    n_{\mathrm{dev}}-n_{\mathrm{tr}},
    \\
    n_{\mathrm{te}}
    &=
    N-n_{\mathrm{dev}}.
\end{align}

The chronological blocks are
\begin{align}
    \Dtrain
    &=
    \{1,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{tr}}\},
    \\
    \Dcal
    &=
    \{n_{\mathrm{tr}}+1,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{dev}}\},
    \\
    \Dtest
    &=
    \{n_{\mathrm{dev}}+1,\ldots,N\}.
\end{align}

The readout parameters are estimated only from $\Dtrain$. The OOB gate,
FastSketch shrinkage, and initial conformity-score buffer are estimated only
from $\Dcal$. Test observations are reserved for final rolling evaluation.
For every $t\in\Dtest$, the model produces
\begin{equation}
    \left(
    \yhat_{t|t-1},
    \widehat{L}_t,
    \widehat{U}_t
    \right)
    =
    \mathcal{A}
    \left(
    y_{1:t-1};
    \bm{\theta}_{m,d}^{\star}
    \right),
    \label{eq:rolling_prediction}
\end{equation}
with horizon $H=1$ and rolling step equal to one.
The causality requirement is
\begin{equation}
    \yhat_{t|t-1},
    \widehat{L}_t,
    \widehat{U}_t
    \in
    \Fcal_{t-1}.
    \label{eq:measurability}
\end{equation}

After the prediction is recorded, $y_t$ is revealed and used only to compute the test error, to compute the nonconformity score $s_t$, and to update the ACI state for time $t+1$.
Under the additional requirement that all preprocessing parameters are fitted
on $\Dtrain$ only, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:rolling_prediction}--\eqref{eq:measurability}
exclude look-ahead bias by construction.
The readout and gate remain frozen throughout $\Dtest$. Only the causal state,
prior history, and ACI buffer are updated sequentially.

\subsection{Prediction Intervals for Reference Models}
\label{subsec:baseline_intervals}

For models that provide native predictive quantiles, including probabilistic
ARIMA, ETS, GP, and Chronos configurations, the corresponding nominal
$90\%$ intervals are used.
For deterministic baselines, a common post-hoc interval is constructed from
calibration residuals:
\begin{equation}
    u_t
    =
    y_t-\yhat_{t|t-1},
    \qquad
    t\in\Dcal.
\end{equation}

The calibration standard deviation is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{cal}}
    =
    \sqrt{
    \frac{1}{|\Dcal|-1}
    \sum_{t\in\Dcal}
    \left(
    u_t-\overline{u}_{\mathrm{cal}}
    \right)^2
    }.
\end{equation}

The resulting reference interval is
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{C}^{\,\mathrm{Gauss}}_t
    =
    \left[
    \yhat_{t|t-1}
    -
    z_{1-\delta/2}\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{cal}},
    \;
    \yhat_{t|t-1}
    +
    z_{1-\delta/2}\widehat{\sigma}_{\mathrm{cal}}
    \right],
    \label{eq:baseline_gaussian_interval}
\end{equation}
where $z_{0.95}\approx1.645$ for $\delta=0.10$.
This construction enables a common interval-score comparison, but it is not
presented as theoretically equivalent to adaptive conformal inference.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics and Protocol}
\label{subsec:metrics}

Let
\begin{equation}
    \left\{
    \left(
    y_t,
    \yhat_t,
    \widehat{L}_t,
    \widehat{U}_t
    \right)
    \right\}_{t=1}^{T}
\end{equation}
denote the forecasts and intervals over the final test block.

\subsubsection{Mean Squared Error}

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{MSE}
    =
    \frac{1}{T}
    \sum_{t=1}^{T}
    \left(
    y_t-\yhat_t
    \right)^2.
    \label{eq:mse}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Root Mean Squared Error}

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{RMSE}
    =
    \sqrt{
    \frac{1}{T}
    \sum_{t=1}^{T}
    \left(
    y_t-\yhat_t
    \right)^2
    }.
    \label{eq:rmse}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Coefficient of Determination}

Let
\begin{equation}
    \overline{y}
    =
    \frac{1}{T}
    \sum_{t=1}^{T}y_t.
\end{equation}

Then
\begin{equation}
    R^2
    =
    1
    -
    \frac{
        \displaystyle
        \sum_{t=1}^{T}
        \left(
        y_t-\yhat_t
        \right)^2
    }{
        \displaystyle
        \sum_{t=1}^{T}
        \left(
        y_t-\overline{y}
        \right)^2
    }.
    \label{eq:r2}
\end{equation}

When the test variance is zero, $R^2$ is undefined and is not used for ranking.

\subsubsection{Epsilon-Stabilized MAPE}

To prevent division by zero or numerical instability near zero-valued targets,
we define
\begin{equation}
    \MAPEe
    =
    \frac{100}{T}
    \sum_{t=1}^{T}
    \frac{
        \left|
        y_t-\yhat_t
        \right|
    }{
        \max
        \left(
        |y_t|,
        \eps
        \right)
    },
    \qquad
    \eps=1\times10^{-5}.
    \label{eq:mape_eps}
\end{equation}

The factor $100$ expresses the metric as a percentage.

\subsubsection{Empirical Coverage Probability}

At nominal coverage $1-\delta$,
\begin{equation}
    \ECP(1-\delta)
    =
    \frac{1}{T}
    \sum_{t=1}^{T}
    \Ind
    \left\{
    y_t
    \in
    [\widehat{L}_t,\widehat{U}_t]
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:ecp}
\end{equation}

Coverage quality is evaluated through deviation from the target:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{\mathrm{cov}}
    =
    \left|
    \ECP(1-\delta)-(1-\delta)
    \right|.
    \label{eq:coverage_deviation}
\end{equation}

Coverage values above the target are not automatically interpreted as superior,
because they may result from excessively wide intervals.

\subsubsection{Interval Width}

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{IW}
    =
    \frac{1}{T}
    \sum_{t=1}^{T}
    \left(
    \widehat{U}_t-\widehat{L}_t
    \right).
    \label{eq:interval_width}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Interval Score}

For miscoverage $\delta$, the interval score is
\begin{align}
    \mathrm{IS}_{\delta,t}
    &=
    \left(
    \widehat{U}_t-\widehat{L}_t
    \right)
    \nonumber\\
    &\quad+
    \frac{2}{\delta}
    \left(
    \widehat{L}_t-y_t
    \right)
    \Ind
    \left\{
    y_t<\widehat{L}_t
    \right\}
    \nonumber\\
    &\quad+
    \frac{2}{\delta}
    \left(
    y_t-\widehat{U}_t
    \right)
    \Ind
    \left\{
    y_t>\widehat{U}_t
    \right\}.
    \label{eq:interval_score}
\end{align}

The first term penalizes width, whereas the remaining terms penalize misses
below and above the interval.

\subsubsection{Seasonal Scaling Factor}

For monthly seasonality $m=12$, the in-sample seasonal-naive scale is
\begin{equation}
    s_m
    =
    \frac{1}{
    n_{\mathrm{tr}}-m
    }
    \sum_{t=m+1}^{n_{\mathrm{tr}}}
    \left|
    y_t-y_{t-m}
    \right|.
    \label{eq:seasonal_scale}
\end{equation}

A numerical safeguard is applied as
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{s}_m
    =
    \max(s_m,\eps_s).
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Mean Scaled Interval Score}

The MSIS is
\begin{equation}
    \MSIS_{\delta}
    =
    \frac{1}{T}
    \sum_{t=1}^{T}
    \frac{
    \mathrm{IS}_{\delta,t}
    }{
    \widetilde{s}_m
    }.
    \label{eq:msis}
\end{equation}

Lower MSIS indicates a more favorable joint balance between interval sharpness
and coverage.

\subsubsection{Operational Runtime and Trainable Parameters}

Operational runtime is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Time}_{\mathrm{op}}
    =
    t_{\mathrm{end}}
    -
    t_{\mathrm{start}},
    \label{eq:operational_time}
\end{equation}
where timing begins immediately before final fitting with the stored
configuration and ends after the test forecasts are produced.
Trainable-parameter counts include coefficients estimated from the target
series but exclude frozen foundation-model parameters, fixed ESN matrices, and fixed FastSketch filters.
Accordingly, parameter count measures target-domain statistical complexity
rather than total memory footprint.

\subsection{Reproducibility Controls}
\label{subsec:reproducibility}

The following controls are applied throughout the experiments:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item all dataset and model random seeds are fixed and recorded;

    \item public-series identifiers and truncation lengths are stored in
    configuration files;

    \item dataset-level HPO configurations are serialized before final testing;

    \item no test observation is used for feature scaling, model selection,
    gate calibration, or initialization of the conformal buffer;

    \item neural models are trained without temporal shuffling and with
    validation-based early stopping;

    % \item unsuccessful executions are logged with their failure reason and are
    % excluded rather than replaced by arbitrary error values;

    \item point forecasts and uncertainty intervals are evaluated on the
    original data scale;

    \item operational runtime and one-time offline HPO cost are reported
    separately.
\end{enumerate}

This protocol ensures that the empirical comparison evaluates frozen
dataset-level configurations under a common strictly causal forecasting
workflow, while preserving the distinction between predictive accuracy,
adaptive conformal uncertainty estimation, and operational computational cost.

\section{Conclusions}



This study introduced S3-Forecaster and S3-FastSketch, two gradient-free residual-adaptation architectures for engineering time series with fewer than 200 observations. Both methods preserve a frozen causal forecasting prior and learn only a regularized correction of its residual errors. S3-Forecaster represents the residual dynamics through a fixed Echo State Network, a convex Bayesian Ridge readout, and a volatility-sensitive out-of-bag gate, whereas S3-FastSketch replaces the reservoir with a deterministic causal multiscale representation and projected calibration shrinkage. Adaptive Conformal Inference provides a common distribution-free uncertainty layer without assuming a parametric residual distribution or requiring end-to-end neural fine-tuning.

The empirical evidence supports dataset- and objective-dependent model selection rather than universal superiority. In the ICMD engineering application, S3-Forecaster achieved the lowest model-scale RMSE and the highest $R^2$, while S3-FastSketch produced the lowest proportional point error and Chronos obtained the most favorable MSIS. On M4, S3-FastSketch was the strongest point forecaster across RMSE, $R^2$, and $\mathrm{MAPE}_{\varepsilon}$, whereas S3-Forecaster yielded the best adaptive conformal interval score. CIF favored the reservoir-based variant, Tourism favored the compact sketch representation, and M3 constituted a boundary case in which S3-Forecaster matched ETS in point accuracy but exhibited insufficient adaptive conformal coverage. Consequently, the appropriate S3 variant depends on the residual geometry, the evaluation criterion, and the stability of the calibration regime.

The ablation results clarify the mechanism underlying these outcomes. Residual adaptation is beneficial only when the frozen prior leaves a stable component that is causally predictable from the available residual history. When this condition is not satisfied, the correction may introduce more estimation variance than systematic-error reduction. The out-of-bag gate should therefore be interpreted as protective shrinkage rather than as an independent source of accuracy: it substantially attenuated over-correction in ICMD, behaved approximately as the identity in several favorable regimes, and only partially mitigated negative transfer in CIF. Similarly, the effectiveness of ACI depended on the representativeness of the short calibration block; its sequential update improved interval adaptation in selected cases but did not guarantee exact conditional coverage over test blocks containing only $10$--$24$ observations.

From an operational perspective, the proposed family achieves low target-domain statistical complexity by replacing iterative backpropagation with fixed feature transformations and closed-form convex readouts. S3-Forecaster required a median operational runtime of approximately $0.55\,\mathrm{s}$ and was, in median paired comparisons, approximately $21\times$, $9\times$, and $5\times$ faster than the evaluated LSTM, CNN, and AR-KAN models, respectively. S3-FastSketch reduced the learned adaptation to approximately $24$--$32$ coefficients in the reported experiments, although its wall-clock advantage was not uniform across datasets. Thus, the principal engineering contribution is not absolute minimal latency, but a favorable balance among nonlinear residual representation, controlled trainable complexity, causal uncertainty adaptation, and deployment without neural-network retraining.

Overall, the results position the S3 family as a selective and computationally efficient residual-adaptation framework for short, non-stationary engineering time series. The current evidence remains limited by the small number of evaluated series, discrete empirical coverage estimates, one-step-ahead forecasting, and sensitivity to prior and calibration selection. Future work should therefore evaluate the framework over complete multi-series collections, extend the architecture to multivariate and multi-horizon forecasting, incorporate exogenous engineering variables, and develop diagnostics that estimate residual predictability before activating the adapter. Such extensions would enable automatic selection among the frozen prior, S3-Forecaster, and S3-FastSketch according to the effective information content of the available history.
